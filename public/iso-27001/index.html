ISO 27001:2022 Implementation Plan for Startups & Small Businesses

A practical, sequenced roadmap that gets you from “we should do security” to “we have a functioning ISMS and a certification path” without freezing your team.

Duration: ~ 5–6 months of focused effort
Best for: 10–200 person SaaS, tech, and services companies
How to use this page: Work phase by phase. Assign owners, add target dates, and track completion. This roadmap is designed to be copy-pasted into your PM tool and turned into real work.

Detailed Implementation Plan

Phase 1 – Strategy, Scope & Readiness (Weeks 1–3)

Keep the scope sane, align with your business model, and avoid “ISO theater.”

Objective: Define what’s in scope, why ISO matters to your company, and how you’ll get there. This is where you avoid over-inflating scope and locking yourself into unnecessary work.
Deliverable: ISMS Scope Statement
Deliverable: Gap Assessment Report
Deliverable: ISO Roadmap
  1. Define ISMS scope. Include only products, locations, teams, and systems that support revenue-critical or regulated services. Avoid “whole company” unless your tech stack is truly unified.
  2. Clarify business drivers. Capture the real reasons: enterprise deals, customer requirements, investor expectations, regulatory pressure, or competitive edge.
  3. Run a baseline gap assessment. Compare your current posture against ISO 27001:2022 and Annex A. Identify what already exists (policies, practices, tools) and where you have no coverage.
  4. Build the implementation roadmap. Turn gaps into a 3–6 month task list: owners, dates, dependencies. Load it into your PM tool and treat it like a real delivery project.

Phase 2 – Leadership Commitment & ISMS Foundation (Weeks 3–5)

ISO lives or dies by leadership. In a small company, this is non-negotiable.

Objective: Assign ownership and establish the ISMS as a real management system, not a side project.
Deliverable: InfoSec Policy
Deliverable: ISMS Charter
Deliverable: Governance Calendar
  1. Appoint a CISO / ISMS Lead. This can be internal or fractional, but it must be explicit. They own risk, policies, audits, and ongoing maintenance.
  2. Approve the Information Security Policy. This “constitution” defines scope, responsibilities, risk approach, and leadership’s commitment to continuous improvement.
  3. Establish ISMS governance. Set monthly ISMS syncs, quarterly risk reviews, and an annual management review. Put them on the calendar now.

Phase 3 – Risk Management & SoA (Weeks 5–7)

Your risk process is the engine that justifies every control you implement.

Objective: Turn vague “security concerns” into a structured risk view, and tie Annex A controls to real risks.
Deliverable: Risk Methodology
Deliverable: Risk Register
Deliverable: Statement of Applicability
  1. Define risk methodology. Choose scales for likelihood and impact, define risk levels (low/med/high), and spell out how you treat and accept risk.
  2. Build the risk register. Identify key assets, threats, and vulnerabilities. Document risk, existing controls, treatment plans, and owners.
  3. Create the Statement of Applicability. For each Annex A control, mark it applicable or not and justify any exclusions. Link each control to risks and control owners.

Phase 4 – Policies, Procedures & Controls (Weeks 7–12)

This is where most small companies feel overwhelmed. The key is to only codify what you’ll actually do.

Objective: Implement right-sized controls, mapped to your risks, and wrap them in policies and procedures that match your real workflows.
Deliverable: Core Policy Set
Deliverable: Technical Control Baseline
Deliverable: Operational Procedures
  1. Draft essential policies first. Start with: Acceptable Use, Access Control, Change Management, Incident Response, Business Continuity, Supplier Management, Asset Management, Mobile/Remote Work, Backup, Logging & Monitoring, Cryptography.
  2. Document key procedures. Onboarding/offboarding, dev/change workflows, patching, vulnerability scans, backup testing, endpoint hardening, incident handling, vendor risk reviews.
  3. Implement technical controls. Enforce SSO + MFA, device management (MDM), encrypted storage, centralized logging, automated backups, hardened cloud baseline, secret management, and scheduled vulnerability scans.

Phase 5 – Evidence, Training & Internal Audit (Weeks 12–16)

Without evidence, controls “don’t exist” to an auditor. This is where you prove it.

Objective: Demonstrate that your ISMS is real: people know what to do, and you have records showing that controls run as intended.
Deliverable: Evidence Repository
Deliverable: Training Records
Deliverable: Internal Audit Report
  1. Collect and organize evidence. Screenshots, tickets, logs, meeting minutes, access reviews, backup reports, incident records, vendor assessments. Store them in a structured folder or GRC tool.
  2. Run security awareness training. Short, practical content that covers phishing, data handling, password hygiene, reporting incidents, and remote work practices.
  3. Perform internal audits. Audit ISO clauses and Annex A controls. Document nonconformities and opportunities for improvement with clear corrective actions.

Phase 6 & 7 – Management Review & Certification (Weeks 16–24)

You close the loop internally, then invite the certification body to test you.

Objective: Confirm leadership is fully aware and supportive, then successfully complete Stage 1 and Stage 2 audits.
Deliverable: Management Review Minutes
Deliverable: Audit Readiness Pack
Deliverable: Corrective Action Log
  1. Run management review. Present internal audit results, risk changes, metrics, incidents, supplier issues, and resourcing needs. Record decisions and inputs.
  2. Complete Stage 1 audit. The auditor reviews your documentation to confirm ISMS design and readiness. Address any gaps identified.
  3. Complete Stage 2 audit. The auditor tests operational effectiveness via interviews, samples, and evidence. Fix non-conformities within agreed timelines.

Ongoing – Keeping ISO 27001 Alive

Certification is not the finish line. The standard expects you to maintain and improve.

Objective: Turn ISO 27001 into a normal part of how you run the company – not a yearly scramble.
  1. Quarterly: review risks, KRIs/KPIs, vendor performance, and incident trends.
  2. Semi-annually: perform access reviews and control spot-checks.
  3. Annually: run internal audit, management review, business continuity test, and incident response tabletop exercise.
  4. Continuously: tune controls as the business scales, new products launch, or new threats emerge.
