Why Most SOC 2 Projects Become Documentation Theater

An engaging, thought-provoking article exploring why many SOC 2 efforts look organized on the surface while failing to build a credible control environment underneath.

There is a predictable moment in a lot of SOC 2 efforts when the project starts to look busy, organized, and expensive — but not meaningfully more secure.

Policies are being written. Screenshots are being saved. Someone is chasing evidence in Slack. A compliance platform has been purchased. A weekly meeting exists. Everyone says progress is happening.

And yet, underneath the visible activity, the control environment is still thin. Ownership is unclear. Decisions that should have been made at the leadership level remain unresolved. Evidence is being collected before anyone has decided what the evidence is supposed to prove. The result is not readiness. It is performance. It is documentation theater.

That distinction matters because SOC 2 is not a report about whether a company can generate paperwork. It is an examination of controls relevant to one or more Trust Services Criteria, and for a Type 2 report, the auditor evaluates not just control design but operating effectiveness over a defined period. In other words, the report is about whether controls exist and function, not whether the company can assemble a folder of artifacts at the last minute. (aicpa-cima.com)

Documentation theater is a management failure disguised as a compliance project

Most companies do not set out to create theater. They get there because they misunderstand what the work is.

They treat SOC 2 as a documentation project when it is really a control design and operating discipline project. Documentation matters, but documentation is downstream. If the underlying operating model is weak, the documents become a thin narrative stretched over inconsistent practices.

That is why so many efforts start to feel strangely detached from the business. Teams produce policies they do not operationalize. They assign owners who do not actually own the process. They collect evidence of activities that are informal, inconsistent, or newly invented for the audit window. Readiness becomes a kind of administrative reenactment of good governance rather than good governance itself.

Current readiness guidance from major compliance vendors reinforces this point, even when their tone is more diplomatic: readiness involves controls, documentation, scope, evidence, and preparation before the formal audit, and evidence must be organized around real control activity rather than assembled as an afterthought. (vanta.com)

The first cause: leadership delegates the project before making the hard decisions

A SOC 2 project often goes wrong before the first policy draft is written.

Leadership decides the company “needs SOC 2,” then pushes the effort downward without resolving the questions that shape the whole program:

  • What is actually in scope?
  • Which Trust Services Criteria matter now?
  • What customer, revenue, or deal pressure is driving this?
  • What systems, vendors, and teams are genuinely material?
  • What level of control maturity is realistic in the next six to twelve months?
  • Who has decision rights when the compliance need conflicts with speed or convenience?

When those decisions stay vague, the team compensates with motion. They produce documents because documents are visible. They schedule meetings because meetings feel like governance. They over-collect evidence because evidence feels safer than judgment.

But ambiguity at the top cannot be solved by paperwork at the bottom.

The second cause: companies confuse templates with controls

A template is not a control.

A policy is not proof that an activity happens.

A spreadsheet full of assigned tasks is not governance.

This should be obvious, but it is one of the most common distortions in early-stage readiness work. Teams buy or download a starter library, populate it with their company name, and feel temporary relief because the program now looks more complete. Yet the core question remains unanswered: do the actual practices, approvals, reviews, restrictions, escalations, and records exist in a way that would support examination?

The AICPA frames SOC engagements around controls over systems and information, not around the existence of documents alone. Type 2 reporting in particular is about whether controls operated effectively over time. A well-written policy may help explain a control, but it does not substitute for the control. (aicpa-cima.com)

This is where documentation theater becomes seductive. Templates create the appearance of maturity quickly. Control operation creates maturity slowly.

One looks finished. The other is real.

The third cause: evidence collection starts before evidence logic exists

A surprising amount of SOC 2 pain comes from this sequence error.

Teams begin collecting evidence before they have clearly mapped:

  1. the control objective,
  2. the activity that satisfies it,
  3. the owner responsible,
  4. the cadence on which it occurs,
  5. and the artifact that would reasonably demonstrate operation.

Without that logic, evidence gathering becomes scavenging. People save screenshots because screenshots feel safe. They export lists because lists feel formal. They create folders full of materials that may be adjacent to the control but do not clearly demonstrate that the control was designed or operated effectively.

That is why many organizations feel overwhelmed by documentation volume while still feeling unprepared. The problem is not merely quantity. It is traceability.

Readiness guidance from Secureframe and Vanta repeatedly emphasizes identifying scope and objectives, documenting control activities, and ensuring evidence availability in a structured way. That order matters. Evidence should emerge from the control structure, not from fear. (secureframe.com)

The fourth cause: ownership is nominal, not operational

In weak SOC 2 projects, ownership is assigned as a label rather than as an operating responsibility.

Someone is “the owner” of access reviews, but they do not control the systems involved, the review cadence, or the escalation path. Someone “owns” vendor management, but no one has defined review thresholds, evidence expectations, or remediation triggers. Security ownership, IT ownership, engineering ownership, and compliance ownership blur together until everyone is vaguely involved and no one is fully accountable.

This is one reason smaller companies often struggle more than expected. The issue is not size by itself. It is that small companies rely on overlapping roles, informal communication, and founder-driven exception handling. Those habits can work operationally for a time. They do not translate cleanly into an auditable control environment without deliberate design.

The solution is not to create bureaucratic theater. It is to define what must be owned, by whom, on what cadence, and with what evidence trail.

The fifth cause: automation is purchased before the process is understood

Automation platforms can be useful. They can reduce manual evidence collection, centralize workflows, and help keep control monitoring organized. But they do not rescue a company from weak decisions.

If the scope is wrong, automation scales the wrong scope.

If ownership is unclear, automation gives unclear owners more tasks.

If the control model is thin, automation creates a more efficient way to monitor thin controls.

The compliance platforms themselves implicitly acknowledge this by surrounding their products with readiness assessments, scoping guidance, audit preparation steps, and documentation planning. The product is not the program. It supports the program. (vanta.com)

This is where many teams lose money early. They buy the machinery of readiness before they have built the logic of readiness.

The sixth cause: the company treats SOC 2 as an isolated event

SOC 2 projects deteriorate into theater when the organization thinks the goal is to “get the report” rather than improve the reliability of the operating environment.

That mindset creates predictable behaviors:

  • short-term fixes instead of sustainable routines,
  • temporary evidence drives instead of recurring operating cadence,
  • one-off policy writing instead of governance,
  • audit-window behavior instead of year-round discipline.

A Type 2 report is especially unforgiving of this mentality because operating effectiveness is assessed over a period of time. That structure naturally favors organizations that treat controls as part of management practice rather than seasonal activity. (drata.com)

The companies that handle SOC 2 well usually do something subtle but important: they use the project to force clarity into the way the business already runs. They do not build a parallel compliance universe. They tighten the real one.

What serious readiness looks like instead

Serious readiness is less glamorous than theater, but far more valuable.

It usually has these characteristics:

A narrow, explicit scope.
The company knows which systems, teams, vendors, and criteria matter now, and which do not.

A control model tied to real operations.
Controls are connected to actual practices, not idealized ones copied from a library.

Clear ownership.
Each significant control or process has an accountable owner with the authority to make it happen.

Evidence logic.
The team understands what artifact demonstrates which activity, why it matters, and how it will be retained.

Leadership involvement.
Not daily micromanagement, but real decision-making around scope, tradeoffs, and priorities.

A roadmap that respects sequence.
Scoping before tooling, ownership before evidence accumulation, process before polish.

That is the difference between a company preparing for examination and a company preparing for appearance.

The harder truth: documentation theater is expensive

Theater feels cheaper than control design because templates are fast and visible. In reality, it is often more expensive.

It burns time in rework. It causes confusion across teams. It creates false confidence before customer diligence conversations. It leads organizations to believe they are close when they are structurally early. And when the auditor asks reasonable questions that connect documents to operation, the gap becomes visible very quickly.

What looked like progress turns out to be inventory.

That is why the most useful early question in a SOC 2 effort is not, “What documents do we need?”

It is, “What must actually be true, repeatedly, in the business, for these controls to be credible?”

That question shifts the project out of theater and back into management.

A better way to think about the work

SOC 2 should not be approached as a library-building exercise. It should be approached as a structured effort to make parts of the organization more deliberate, more reviewable, and more trustworthy.

That means:

  • define scope clearly,
  • choose a realistic path,
  • assign actual owners,
  • build controls around the way the company operates,
  • collect evidence that proves operation,
  • and use documentation to describe reality rather than invent it.

When companies do that, the documents still matter. They just stop being the performance.

They become what they should have been all along: the written record of a control environment that actually exists.
