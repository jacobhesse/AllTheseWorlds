Chemistry I Course Topics

Chemistry I, often called General Chemistry I, introduces the foundational concepts needed to understand matter, chemical reactions, quantitative problem-solving, and the principles that govern atomic and molecular behavior. This page outlines the topics commonly taught in a first-semester university chemistry course.

What Is Chemistry I?

A typical university Chemistry I course is designed for science, engineering, health, and related majors. It emphasizes conceptual understanding, mathematical reasoning, and laboratory skills. Students usually learn how to interpret chemical behavior at both the microscopic level of atoms and molecules and the macroscopic level of observable substances and reactions.

1. Matter and Measurement

Chemistry begins with the classification of matter and the measurement systems used to describe it quantitatively.

  • States of matter: solid, liquid, gas
  • Physical vs. chemical properties
  • Physical vs. chemical changes
  • Elements, compounds, and mixtures
  • Units, dimensional analysis, and significant figures
  • Density and temperature scales

2. Atomic Structure

Students learn how modern chemistry is built on the atomic theory of matter and how subatomic particles determine chemical identity and behavior.

  • Historical development of atomic theory
  • Protons, neutrons, and electrons
  • Atomic number and mass number
  • Isotopes and average atomic mass
  • Structure of the nucleus

3. The Periodic Table and Periodic Trends

The periodic table organizes elements according to recurring patterns in their properties and electron arrangements.

  • Groups, periods, metals, nonmetals, metalloids
  • Valence electrons
  • Atomic radius
  • Ionization energy
  • Electron affinity
  • Electronegativity
  • Predicting chemical reactivity from position in the table

4. Electronic Structure of Atoms

Chemistry I usually includes the quantum-based model of the atom and how electrons occupy orbitals.

  • Electromagnetic radiation and light
  • Bohr model and line spectra
  • Wave-particle duality
  • Quantum numbers
  • Atomic orbitals: s, p, d, f
  • Electron configurations and orbital diagrams

5. Chemical Bonding

Bonding explains how atoms combine to form stable substances and why compounds have specific structures and properties.

  • Ionic bonding
  • Covalent bonding
  • Metallic bonding
  • Lewis structures
  • Octet rule and common exceptions
  • Bond polarity
  • Introduction to molecular geometry and VSEPR theory

6. Chemical Nomenclature and Formulas

Students learn the rules for naming chemical compounds and writing correct formulas from names or ion charges.

  • Naming ionic compounds
  • Naming molecular compounds
  • Polyatomic ions
  • Acids and their naming conventions
  • Writing empirical and molecular formulas

7. Stoichiometry

Stoichiometry is the mathematical framework used to quantify chemical reactions. It is one of the core quantitative topics in Chemistry I.

  • The mole concept
  • Avogadro’s number
  • Molar mass calculations
  • Converting between grams, moles, and particles
  • Balancing chemical equations
  • Limiting reactants and excess reactants
  • Theoretical yield, actual yield, and percent yield

8. Chemical Reactions

Chemistry I typically surveys common reaction types and teaches students how to predict products and interpret reaction behavior.

  • Synthesis reactions
  • Decomposition reactions
  • Single-replacement reactions
  • Double-replacement reactions
  • Combustion reactions
  • Precipitation reactions
  • Acid-base neutralization
  • Oxidation-reduction introduction

9. Solutions and Concentration

Many Chemistry I courses introduce solutions as homogeneous mixtures and cover ways to quantify dissolved substances.

  • Solute and solvent
  • Electrolytes vs. nonelectrolytes
  • Molarity
  • Dilution calculations
  • Solution preparation
  • Solubility basics

10. Thermochemistry

Thermochemistry examines energy changes that accompany physical and chemical processes.

  • System and surroundings
  • Heat and temperature
  • Endothermic and exothermic processes
  • Specific heat and calorimetry
  • Enthalpy changes
  • Heating and cooling calculations

11. Gases and Gas Laws

Gas behavior provides one of the clearest examples of the relationship between microscopic particle motion and macroscopic physical behavior.

  • Pressure and its measurement
  • Boyle’s law
  • Charles’s law
  • Gay-Lussac’s law
  • Combined gas law
  • Ideal gas law
  • Gas stoichiometry basics
  • Kinetic molecular theory

12. Laboratory Skills and Scientific Practice

University Chemistry I usually includes a laboratory component that develops hands-on skills, data interpretation, and scientific communication.

  • Laboratory safety procedures
  • Proper use of balances, glassware, and volumetric tools
  • Data collection and uncertainty
  • Graphing and interpreting results
  • Writing lab reports
  • Observing reactions and identifying evidence of change

How These Topics Fit Together

A well-designed Chemistry I course usually progresses from basic description of matter to atomic and molecular explanation, and then to quantitative prediction. The sequence often looks like this:

  • Learn what matter is and how chemists measure it
  • Understand atoms, elements, and the periodic table
  • Study how atoms bond and form compounds
  • Use formulas and equations to describe reactions
  • Apply mathematics through stoichiometry and gas law problems
  • Connect chemistry to energy changes and laboratory evidence

Typical Learning Outcomes

By the end of Chemistry I, students are commonly expected to be able to:

  • Classify matter and interpret measurements correctly
  • Explain atomic structure and periodic behavior
  • Name compounds and write chemical formulas
  • Balance equations and solve stoichiometric problems
  • Describe basic bonding and molecular structure
  • Calculate energy or gas-law relationships in chemical systems
  • Apply safe and accurate laboratory methods
